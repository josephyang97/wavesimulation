Guided investigation: the displacement–distance (y–x) graph

Work through the tasks in order. Press Set up this task to load the starting values, then follow the protocol. Write your own answer first, then open “Check your answer”.
Observation protocol (use every time)
  1. Check the axes first. The y–x graph has distance on the horizontal axis; the y–t graph has time. They look alike but answer different questions.
  2. Pause before you measure. A y–x graph is a snapshot of the whole wave at one instant.
  3. Read from the graph, then confirm with the readout. Use the gridlines and tick marks; do not just copy the numbers.
  4. Change one variable at a time and predict the result before you test it.
  5. Link the three views. Find the red particle in the medium, on the y–x graph and on the y–t graph.
Task 1: What does the graph show? (reading A and λ)

Protocol

  1. The wave is paused at t = 0. Look at the green y–x graph and the particles in the medium panel above it. Match the shape of the line to the positions of the particles.
  2. Move the probe slider slowly. Watch the red dot on the graph and the red particle in the medium.
  3. Use the gridlines (0.2 m steps vertically, 0.5 m ticks horizontally) to read values.

Guiding questions

  1. State what each point on the y–x graph represents.
  2. Determine the amplitude from the graph. Which feature do you measure from, and to?
  3. Determine the wavelength from the graph. Why is measuring from one crest to the next better than from the start to the end of the graph?
  4. What is the displacement at x = 0 when t = 0? What does that tell you about where the wave “starts”?
Check your answer

1. The displacement y of the particle at that distance x from the source, at this one instant.
2. A ≈ 0.30 m: the maximum displacement, measured from the equilibrium line (y = 0) to a crest or trough, not crest to trough.
3. λ ≈ 1.2 m (crests near 0.3 m and 1.5 m). Measuring between identical points of adjacent cycles is accurate; the graph width does not have to be a whole number of wavelengths.
4. y = 0 at x = 0, t = 0 because the wave has sin form here. The graph shape depends on the choice of t = 0; it is not a property of the wave.

Task 2: A snapshot of a moving wave (graph shift and wave speed)

Protocol

  1. At t = 0, note the x-position of the first crest on the y–x graph: x₁ = ____ m.
  2. Press Step ten times (0.5 s). Note the clock t and the new crest position x₂ = ____ m.
  3. Calculate Δx/Δt. Compare with the readout v = fλ.
  4. While the wave is playing, watch only the red particle in the medium. Does it travel along with the crest?

Guiding questions

  1. Describe how the shape of the y–x graph changes between the two snapshots.
  2. Calculate the speed of the crest. Does it equal fλ?
  3. In which direction does the crest move? In which direction does the red particle move? Explain why a transverse wave is not “matter flowing to the right”.
  4. Which graph, y–x or y–t, would you use to find λ? To find T?
Check your answer

1. The same shape slides to the right without changing size.
2. Crest moves from ≈ 0.3 m to ≈ 0.6 m in 0.5 s: v ≈ 0.6 m/s, matching fλ = (1/2)(1.2).
3. The crest moves in +x; the particle moves only up and down (perpendicular). The wave transfers energy, not matter.
4. λ from y–x (distance axis), T from y–t (time axis).

Task 3: Predicting particle motion from the graph

Protocol

  1. The wave travels to the right. Imagine the whole green curve sliding a tiny distance to the right. Look at the curve just to the left of the red dot.
  2. Predict: will the red particle move up or down next? Write your prediction.
  3. Press Step once and check. Repeat with the probe at x = 0.45 m (use the probe slider, then press Reset).

Guiding questions

  1. Predict the direction of motion of the particle at x = 0.15 m, then at x = 0.45 m.
  2. Explain your prediction using the shape of the graph to the left of the particle.
  3. What would change if the wave travelled to the left?
Check your answer

1. At 0.15 m the particle moves down; at 0.45 m it moves up.
2. The wave moves right, so the particle soon takes the displacement that the point just to its left has now. At 0.15 m the curve to the left is lower; at 0.45 m (just past the crest) it is higher.
3. You would look to the right of the particle instead, so both predictions reverse.

Task 4: Where is a particle fastest, and where is it momentarily at rest?

Protocol

  1. With the probe at the crest (x = 0.3 m), read y and vᵧ from the readouts. Note the length of the blue velocity arrow.
  2. Move the probe to x = 0.6 m (on the equilibrium line). Read y, vᵧ and the arrow again.
  3. Try x = 0.9 m (trough). Compare with the “Max particle speed” readout.

Guiding questions

  1. Compare the speed of the particle at a crest, at y = 0 and at a trough.
  2. How does the steepness of the y–x graph at the probe relate to the particle’s speed?
  3. Is the particle’s velocity the same thing as the wave speed? Justify.
Check your answer

1. At crest and trough vᵧ = 0 (momentary rest, y = ±A). At y = 0, speed is maximum: ωA = 2πA/T ≈ 0.94 m/s.
2. The steeper the curve at the particle, the faster it moves; the graph is flat at a crest and steepest at y = 0.
3. No. Particle speed varies with time, is perpendicular to the travel direction and depends on A and T; the wave speed is constant (0.6 m/s here) and depends on λ and T.

Task 5: What changes the y–x graph? (A, λ and T)

Protocol

  1. Pause at t = 0. Predict first, then change only one slider: A, then λ, then T. Press Reset after each change.
  2. For each, record what happened to the y–x graph: height, spacing, or nothing.
  3. Record λ and T, calculate v = λ/T and compare with the readout. Try two different pairs.

Guiding questions

  1. State which feature of the y–x graph each variable controls.
  2. When you change T, the paused y–x graph looks identical. Explain why T still matters.
  3. Halve T keeping λ fixed. Predict what happens to f and v.
Check your answer

1. A sets the height, λ sets the horizontal spacing of crests; T does not change the snapshot.
2. T controls how quickly the pattern moves and how quickly each particle oscillates (visible in the y–t graph and the speed readout).
3. f doubles and v doubles (v = fλ with λ fixed).

Task 6 (extension): Phase difference between two particles

Protocol

  1. Place the probe at x = 0.3 m. Play and watch its up-and-down motion on the y–t graph, then pause. Note y and vᵧ.
  2. Repeat with the probe at x = 0.9 m, then x = 1.5 m, starting from the same clock time each time (Reset, then Play for the same number of seconds, or Step the same number of times).
  3. Compare the three y–t graphs.

Guiding questions

  1. Identify which pairs of particles move in step and which move oppositely.
  2. How is the distance between particles related to the phase difference? Complete: “particles λ apart are ____; particles λ/2 apart are ____.”
  3. Use the y–x graph to justify your answer to question 2.
Check your answer

1. x = 0.3 m and 1.5 m (λ apart) move in step; x = 0.3 m and 0.9 m (λ/2 apart) move oppositely.
2. λ apart: in phase (phase difference 2π); λ/2 apart: in antiphase (phase difference π).
3. On the y–x graph, points one wavelength apart have identical displacement and slope; points half a wavelength apart have equal and opposite displacement.

Common misconceptions to watch for
  • “The graph shows the path of the wave.” It does not: it shows displacement of many particles at one instant.
  • “Particles move along with the wave.” They oscillate about a fixed position.
  • “Amplitude is crest to trough.” It is the maximum displacement from equilibrium.
  • “A changes the wavelength.” It changes only the height.
Notice: the wave moves right at v = fλ, but each particle only moves up and down. Particle speed is greatest as it passes the equilibrium line (y = 0) and zero at the peaks. The probe's y–t graph has the same shape as the y–x graph, but its horizontal axis measures T instead of λ.
